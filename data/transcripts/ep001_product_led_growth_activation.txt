The Secret to Product-Led Growth: Activation is Everything | Lenny's Podcast

In this episode, Lenny Rachitsky speaks with Elena Vance, Head of Growth at a Series B SaaS company, about why activation — not acquisition — is the real lever for sustainable product-led growth.

Most companies obsess over acquisition funnels. They spend enormous resources on paid ads, content marketing, and SEO, only to watch users sign up, poke around for a few minutes, and then disappear forever. The dirty secret of PLG is that if your activation rate is broken, no amount of top-of-funnel spend will save you. You're filling a leaky bucket.

Elena defines activation precisely: it's the moment a user experiences the core value of your product for the first time. Not the moment they sign up. Not the moment they complete onboarding. The moment they genuinely feel, "Okay, I get it. This is useful to me." For Slack, that's sending 2,000 messages. For Dropbox, that's putting one file in a shared folder and seeing it appear on another device.

**The activation metric should be tied to verified value delivery, not proxy behaviors.** A lot of teams make the mistake of measuring account creation, profile completion, or tutorial completion as their activation metric. These feel good because they're easy to hit, but they have almost no correlation with long-term retention.

The right activation metric is the one that, when users hit it, predicts they'll still be using your product 30 days later. You find it by running cohort analysis: look at users who retained at Day 30 and work backward. What did they all do in the first session that disengaged users didn't? That behavior is your activation event.

Elena shared a framework she uses called the Critical Path Audit. List every step a user has to complete between signing up and reaching your activation event. Then ruthlessly eliminate every step that isn't strictly necessary. If you're asking for credit card info before the user has seen any value, that step is costing you activations. If you're showing a 12-step onboarding wizard before users can use the core feature, you're leaking users at every step.

**Reducing friction in the critical path is often more impactful than improving the destination.** She gave an example: her team cut one field from a form, and activation improved by 17%. Nobody changed the product. Nobody changed the messaging. They just removed one ask that wasn't earning its keep.

The second major framework Elena uses is Progressive Disclosure. Rather than front-loading all setup — integrations, team invites, customization — she advocates deferring every secondary setup task until after the user has experienced core value. Let them experience the magic first, then show them how to make it their own.

**Progressive onboarding is not about skipping steps. It's about sequencing them correctly.** The goal is to get the user to their first moment of genuine value as fast as possible, then build relationship and complexity around that.

Elena's team also does what she calls Activation Interviews — not NPS surveys, not metrics dashboards, but actual 30-minute calls with users who activated and users who didn't, within their first week. The qualitative signal from these conversations consistently surfaces things that quantitative funnels can't. Things like: "I couldn't figure out where to go next," or "I didn't realize that feature existed," or "I thought I needed to invite my team first but I didn't."

On the topic of PLG versus sales-led growth, Elena is nuanced. She doesn't believe PLG is a universal answer. It works best when the product's core value is immediately demonstrable without human explanation, and when individual users can experience that value before needing buy-in from a manager or IT department. Figma works because a designer can open a link and immediately see a beautiful, interactive design file. Notion works because a person can set up a personal workspace in 10 minutes. Products that require weeks of implementation, heavy data migration, or executive sign-off before delivering value are poor candidates for PLG, regardless of how much the market is hyping the motion.

**The honest question every founder should ask is: can a user experience our core value in under 5 minutes, alone, without help?** If the answer is no, PLG is not your primary growth motion — at least not yet.

Lenny asked about the most common mistake he sees PLG companies make. Elena's answer was immediate: confusing engagement with activation. They run A/B tests on onboarding copy, in-app tooltips, and email drip sequences, and they measure success by engagement rates — click-throughs, tooltip dismissals, email opens — without ever checking whether those changes actually moved the activation rate. You can have a highly engaging onboarding flow that completely fails to activate users, because the engagement metrics and the activation metric are measuring different things.

She recommends tying every experiment's success metric directly to the activation event, not to proxies. If a new onboarding step doesn't improve the percentage of users reaching the activation event within 7 days, it failed, regardless of how much users engaged with it.

The episode closed with a question about team structure. Elena runs a small, embedded activation team — two engineers, one designer, one data analyst, herself — that sits between product and growth. They own the end-to-end experience from signup to activation event and are the only team measured purely on the activation rate metric. Everyone else is measured on downstream outcomes (revenue, retention, expansion), but this team is laser-focused on the one metric that makes all the downstream metrics possible.

**Key Takeaway:** Activation is the multiplier on every other growth investment. Fix it first. Measure it precisely. Reduce the friction on the critical path. Defer everything that isn't necessary to reach first value. And interview your users — the answers are there, but they're not in your dashboard.
